About the levels

The numbers are targets, not ratings. No level has been played against rated humans, so a level is where a setting is aimed rather than a strength anyone measured. The dials shown are what actually changes.

Self-play, six games a pair with colours alternated: level 800 beat level 0 six times out of six, and level 1600 scored 5½/6 against 800, so the scale does climb. Neighbouring levels were not separated — a hundred-point gap is about a 64% expected score, and no affordable number of games tells that from a coin — so the step between, say, 900 and 1000 is a design choice. If a level feels wrong, move up or down until games are close and ignore the number.

The strength estimate on an analysed game comes from a second measurement (5 September 2026): eight levels from 0 to 2100 each played two games against themselves, and every game was analysed at each of the three depths — 96 analyses. Average loss per move falls with the level closely enough to fit a line through the logarithm (R² 0.75 to 0.81) up to about 1200; above that every level looks the same to the analyser, so the estimate stops there and says "or above".

    How this is measured

    Your games are compared with the lines below. The opening named is the one the game followed furthest, and transposing into another line you know does not count as leaving. The cost is two searches of equal depth, one after your move and one after the book's — and it is allowed to be nothing.

    How to learn an opening

    Most opening study fails the same way: you memorise ten moves, your opponent deviates on move three, and you are on your own with no idea what the position wanted. The fix is fewer moves and more reasons.

    1. One answer to each first move, not five. You will meet 1.e4 and 1.d4 most of the time. One reply to each, played every game, teaches the resulting positions faster than a broad repertoire — because you keep reaching them.
    2. Know the point of every move before the order. If you can say what a move does — takes a square, opens a line, stops an idea — you can find it again when the move order changes.
    3. Stop at the first position you understand. Six to ten moves is plenty. Beyond that you are memorising preparation for positions you will not reach.
    4. Have an answer for the cheap attacks. Below about 1200 most games are decided by an early raid on f7 or f2, not by theory. Each line lists what goes wrong and what to do about it.
    5. Test yourself on the board. Recognising a move is not the same as finding it. "Test me" plays the other side and makes you produce the move, then spaces the line out over days.
    6. Check your own games against it. The panel above lists the moves where you left these lines, most frequent first, with what each cost.

    Every line and branch has been checked move by move against this app's engine, and none leaves you worse than about half a point.

      About the commentary

      Three sources. While the game is still in one of the app's openings, that opening's own note for the move just played. On a famous game, at the moves that have one, a sentence written about that exact move. And after every move, a search deeper than the one that played it — if it preferred something else, the gap is given in points. What you will not get is what the player was thinking; nothing here can know that.

      A recorded checkmate has to be checkmate on the board or the build fails. A resignation is a fact about a room, not a position, and the screen says so rather than pretending the board proves it.

      Squares come first: files are lettered a to h from White's left, ranks numbered 1 to 8 from White's side. Every square has one name, and it does not change when the board is turned — e4 is e4 from either side. The Vision screen drills those until they are automatic.

      Reading a game

      Moves come in pairs, numbered once: 1. e4 e5 is White's first move and Black's reply. A Black move quoted alone gets three dots — 1… e5. A game ends with its result: 1-0 White won, 0-1 Black won, ½-½ a draw.

      Annotators attach judgements to moves: ! good, !! brilliant, ? a mistake, ?? a blunder, !? interesting, ?! dubious. Those are opinions, not notation. Game Review uses its own marks and says what each one measured.
