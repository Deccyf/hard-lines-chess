What was actually measured

Self-play, six games a pair with colours alternated. Band 800 beat band 0 six times out of six, and band 1600 scored 5½/6 against band 800. So the ladder does climb.

Two things it does not establish. Neighbouring bands were not separated: a hundred-point gap is about a 64% expected score, and no affordable number of games tells that from a coin — so the step between, say, 900 and 1000 is a design choice, not a finding. And the top two bands came out level in that run, because the test capped thinking time at 70ms, which is the very thing that separates them; in ordinary play they get 1800ms and 2400ms.

If a band feels wrong for you, it is: move up or down until the games are close, and ignore the number.

The strength estimate on a reviewed game comes from a second measurement (5 September 2026): eight bands from 0 to 2100 each played two games against themselves, and every game was walked by the reviewer at each of its three settings — 96 reviews. Average loss per move falls with the band's number closely enough to fit a line through the logarithm (R² 0.75 to 0.81), up to about 1200. Above that every band looks the same to the reviewer, because they play the moves it would play, so the estimate stops there and says "or above".

    How to actually learn an opening

    Most opening study fails the same way: you memorise ten moves, your opponent plays the eleventh differently on move three, and you are on your own with no idea what the position wanted. The fix is not more moves. It is fewer moves and more reasons.

    1. Learn one answer to each first move, not five. You will meet 1.e4 and 1.d4 most of the time. One reply to each, played every game, teaches you the resulting positions faster than a broad repertoire ever will — because you keep landing in them.
    2. Know the point of every move before you memorise the order. If you can say what a move does — takes a square, opens a line, stops an idea — you can find it again when the move order changes. If you only know the sequence, you cannot.
    3. Stop at the first position you understand. Six to ten moves is plenty. Past that you are memorising someone else's preparation for positions you will not reach.
    4. Have an answer for the cheap attacks. Below about 1200 most games are decided by an early raid on f7 or f2, not by theory. Each line here lists what actually goes wrong and what to do about it. That section is worth more than the moves.
    5. Test yourself with the board, not the page. Recognising a move when you see it is not the same as finding it. "Test me" plays the other side and makes you produce the move yourself, then spaces the line out over days so it sticks.
    6. Review your own games against it. When you leave the line, that is the interesting moment. Paste the game into Review and see what the engine wanted at the move where you were on your own.

    Every line below, and every branch, has been checked move by move against this app's own engine, and none of them leaves you worse than about half a pawn. Where the text counts something — attackers on a square, a piece with one move — the count was made on the board, not from memory.

    The method

      Why these are graded and your games are not

      King and pawn against king is not estimated here, it is solved: every one of the 393,216 positions is a win or a draw, worked out once and looked up after every move. That is why the app can tell you the move a win turned into a draw rather than only that it did. The search cannot do this — shown the drawn opposition it is confident White is winning — and where the search is the referee instead, the panel above says so.

      Somebody else's game, a move at a time, with the engine saying what it makes of each one.

      Two bands off the ladder, drawn at random, given opposite colours and an opening neither of them picked. Inside the search each side chooses freely among the moves it rates equal, so the same two bands do not play the same game twice. Press it again for another one.

      Why this game

      Games worth watching

      Every move below is replayed through this app's own move generator before it ships, so a game here is the game it says it is. Where the record says checkmate, the final position has to be checkmate or the build fails. Where a player resigned, nothing here can check that — a resignation is a fact about a room, not about a position — and the screen says so rather than pretending the board proves it.

      What the commentary is, and what it is not

      Three sources, in this order. While the moves are still in one of the app's openings, you get that opening's own note for the move just played — the same prose the Openings screen uses, already checked against a board. On a famous game, at the moves that have one, you get a sentence somebody wrote about that exact move. And after every move, a search deeper than the one that played it: if it wanted something else, the gap between the two is printed in pawns.

      What you will not get is what the player was thinking. This app cannot know that, and a confident guess dressed as commentary would be the one thing here that is not measured.

      Where the squares are, without counting up from a1.

      Every other part of this app assumes you can do this. The openings talk about d4 and c5, the review says the mistake was on f7, and the coach answers in algebraic — all of it is noise until the letters and numbers are somewhere you can reach without thinking. Thirty seconds a round.
